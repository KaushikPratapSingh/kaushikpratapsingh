03 — Research

Why I went looking inside a signal nobody had cleaned yet.

A question I carried out of Class 12 became a software pipeline, a verification methodology, a research paper, and a record of everything that went wrong along the way.

I started without a BCI background, a lab, or funding. I had a question: if watches can track speed, heart rate and blood pressure, why shouldn't we eventually be able to observe meaningful changes in the brain too?

Enter the research story
01 — The question

It started as a thought, not a research proposal.

I did not begin with the field. I began with curiosity, then learned the field while trying to make the question actionable.

01
Class 12 · Starting point

Why can't we track the brain the way we track everything else?

I imagined a future where the brain could be monitored as naturally as other physiological signals. I had no prior BCI expertise, so I turned the question into a roadmap: hardware, application, and signal-processing / machine-learning. Without a lab or funding, I chose the part I could actually investigate—software and the processing pipeline—and documented the journey publicly.

“I didn't need to know the field before asking the question. I needed to be willing to learn the field after asking it.”

03 — What I built

A verification-first pipeline, not just an accuracy number.

The research focuses on whether the result survives checking: bootstrap resampling, permutation testing, leave-one-subject-out validation, leakage audits and explicit failure analysis.

02

What it actually does

I built a computational verification pipeline for low-channel EEG motor-imagery classification using Riemannian geometry on covariance matrices. The canonical real-human validation uses a 50-subject leave-one-subject-out cohort, while later stages address the mismatch between research-grade 64-channel wet EEG and the intended 4-channel dry-electrode form factor.

04 — Findings & honesty

The most valuable results were sometimes the ones that broke.

The repository documents errors because an incorrect result that gets silently fixed teaches less than an incorrect result that shows exactly how the failure happened.

The bug that made the paper more honest

76.20% accuracy on pure noise was not a breakthrough.

During audit, Tangent Space Alignment fitted asymmetrically on REST-only trials produced 76.20% accuracy on pure Gaussian noise. That should have been around chance. The v11 correction fitted alignment globally across the training trials and brought pure-noise accuracy down to 51.4%, exactly where it should be.

76.20%Bug · v10
51.4%Fixed · v11
Why Riemannian, not Euclidean

Geometry changes what “average” means.

Naive Euclidean averaging of EEG covariance matrices produced up to 92,898× volume swelling on the same STEW dataset. The Riemannian Fréchet mean conserved the volume with a ratio of 1.000×, respecting the curved geometry of the covariance manifold.

Bug registry

What I caught instead of hiding.

Two checkable entries are shown here; the complete audit trail lives in the repository.

v10 → v11
Tangent Space Alignment temporal leakage. TSA fitted asymmetrically on REST-only trials leaked information across the train/test split, producing 76.20% accuracy on pure Gaussian noise. The correction dropped it to 51.4%.
Seed audit
Random-seed confound. An earlier comparison looked stronger than it was because of an unfixed random seed affecting the split. Re-execution against the raw data caught and corrected it before reporting.

Still in progress

The hardware comes later. The question does not.

The physical device is a future paper when I have access to a lab. For now, this work is evidence that a question can become a serious investigation before the ideal resources arrive. I do not describe the project as finished; I describe what I could establish, what I could not, what broke, and what deserves another paper.

The bigger idea is verification-first research: build, test, find the thing that is wrong, publish what went wrong, and let that failure teach the next version what it needs to become.
