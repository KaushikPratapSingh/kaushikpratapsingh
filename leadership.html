04 — People, practice & pressure

I learned most of this by doing it with other people.

Leadership did not arrive for me as a title. It accumulated through games, rehearsals, school programmes, competitions, disagreements, and the slow realisation that getting everyone to move together is very different from simply knowing where to go.

“Walk with peers and not ahead of them.”
The wall

Before I led people, I learned to teach myself.

When I first tried volleyball in Class 9, I hesitated to play because I did not know how. So I bought a ball and started learning against a wall.

I watched tutorials and repeated the movements at home: toss, receive, pass, serve. I even switched off the fan so the ball would not hit anything unexpectedly. For two years, that wall kept returning the ball. It became my coach. The room filled with marks from the practice, and I still like seeing them because they remind me that progress can leave physical evidence.

The wall never explained what I was doing wrong. It simply gave me another chance to try the same thing better.

In Class 11, when I finally got the chance to play in the Annual Sports Meet, the journey suddenly had an answer: our Green House won the volleyball tournament. I was not the greatest player there. That is not the point. What mattered was going from knowing almost nothing to being good enough to play, compete, and contribute to a winning team.

School President

My first real lesson in leading peers.

I had to learn how to handle pressure without pretending I had all the answers.

01 — Pressure

Make decisions while things are moving.

Programmes, deadlines, volunteers and last-minute changes forced me to act without waiting for perfect certainty.

02 — Contradictions

Bring people toward common ground.

Friends are still people. Leading peers meant negotiating disagreements without turning every difference into a conflict.

03 — Cooperation

Assigning work is not the same as building trust.

I learned that people contribute more willingly when they understand the purpose and feel they are part of it.

04 — Grounding

Walk with people, not ahead of them.

The lesson I carry most is that leadership is participation: organising, contributing, helping, correcting, and sometimes simply being there.

Making together

Sometimes the plan was to make a plan after the plan failed.

I choreographed boys for school programmes from Independence Day to farewell. It was messy. Someone would reject a move at the last moment, someone else would change an idea in rehearsal, and I had to improvise. Digamber was one of the people who pushed me into dancing when I did not want to participate; he was also a good dancer and helped make the whole thing possible.

R

Rangoli — Class 9

I had never made a rangoli before. When I realised nobody wanted to start, two other boys and I made an Indian map. I coloured it with material senior girls gave us.

G

I chose to work on the G20 theme and learned by building the thing rather than waiting to become “good at” it first.

C

Choreography

The real skill was not designing a perfect sequence. It was keeping a group moving when the perfect sequence stopped being possible.

Chess

A different kind of leadership: leading my own decisions.

I played chess as a child, abandoned it, returned to it in Class 11, and this time decided to stay.

When I was around eight, we did not know formal openings, so we invented our own. Mine was based on symmetry. Years later I returned to the game and started learning openings, middlegames and endgames through Chess.com and Lichess. Eventually I moved into competitive play through FIDE Online Arena and earned my first official title: Arena Candidate Master.

The title is not the part that stayed with me. Chess taught me to plan, make decisions under pressure, accept consequences, and review a bad move without pretending it was somebody else's fault.

What remains

I don't really think of these as achievements.

I think of them as places where I learned how I behave when something matters.

The ball, the board, the stage, the school programme, the group that needed organising, the opponent across the net — each gave me a slightly different lesson. The common thread is not winning. It is the willingness to enter something I do not fully know yet, stay with it long enough to learn, and make room for other people while I do.

People changed the shape of my journey.
